# Sequential Thinking

## Overview

This skill solves problems through an explicit chain of thought steps rather than a single shot. Each step builds on, questions, or revises previous steps. It is especially useful when the full shape of the problem is not clear at the start, when the first plan is likely to be wrong, or when the cost of a premature answer is high.

Sequential thinking is not about producing a long internal monologue. It is about maintaining a visible reasoning trail that can be audited, revised, and terminated when sufficient confidence is reached.

## When to Use

- The problem is complex enough that a single answer would hide reasoning
- The problem shape is ambiguous and may change as you understand it
- Multiple options exist and need to be compared before choosing
- You need to surface hidden assumptions before acting
- A plan may need mid-stream revision rather than one-shot execution
- Stakes are high enough that reasoning should be inspectable

**When NOT to use:** The answer is a simple fact, a one-line code change, or the destination is obvious. Don't add reasoning overhead to trivial questions.

## Method

### 1. State the initial problem

Write one clear sentence describing what you are trying to solve or decide. If you cannot write it in one sentence, the problem is likely multiple problems.

### 2. Produce one thought at a time

Each thought should:
- Build on the previous thought, or
- Question it, or
- Revise it

A thought can be:
- An analytical step
- A hypothesis
- A realization
- A question that needs an answer before continuing
- A revision of an earlier conclusion

### 3. Revisability is required, not optional

Mark thoughts that revise previous thinking explicitly. Do not silently overwrite earlier reasoning — show the correction so the trail remains coherent.

### 4. Branch when useful

If two promising paths diverge, label them as branches. Explore each briefly, then decide which branch to pursue. Do not chase every branch indefinitely.

### 5. Generate a solution hypothesis

After enough steps, synthesize the reasoning into a concrete hypothesis or plan.

### 6. Verify the hypothesis

Before finalizing, check:
- Does it address the original problem?
- What evidence supports it?
- What would prove it wrong?
- What are the second-order effects?

### 7. Terminate when confident

Stop when you have enough confidence to act or when further thinking produces diminishing returns. State the final answer clearly.

## Output Format

```markdown
## Problem
[One-sentence statement]

## Thought Chain
1. [First thought]
2. [Second thought — builds on, questions, or revises #1]
3. [Third thought]
...

## Solution Hypothesis
[Concrete proposal]

## Verification
- Supports: [evidence]
- Would refute: [counter-evidence]
- Second-order effects: [list]

## Final Answer
[Clear, actionable conclusion]
```

## Red Flags

- **One-shot answers pretending to be sequential.** If every "thought" is just a section of a pre-written conclusion, the chain is fake.
- **No revisions.** A real reasoning process almost always revises something.
- **Endless expansion.** The goal is to reach confidence, not to produce the longest chain possible.
- **Branch abandonment.** If you introduce a branch, either pursue it or explicitly close it.

## Verification (Pre-Flight)

- [ ] Problem stated in one sentence
- [ ] Each thought connects to at least one previous thought
- [ ] At least one revision or uncertainty is visible, or the problem was genuinely trivial
- [ ] A concrete solution hypothesis is produced
- [ ] Hypothesis is checked against evidence and counter-evidence
- [ ] Final answer is stated explicitly
